On October 15, 2025, a man sat in a glass office in Manhattan's Flatiron Plaza filling out expense reports by hand.

He was Brian Baumgartner, who played Kevin, the accountant on *The Office*. Ramp had named him its "CFO" for the day. His job was to try to keep pace with the 600,000 receipts Ramp automates every day. A live tracker kept score against Ramp's software. He was never going to win, which was the point.

Ramp streamed the whole day, with X as its main stage, booked guests between the paperwork and named the date National Expense Report Day. Ramp counted more than 112 million views across platforms.

Exactly one year later, on October 15, 2026, Ramp announced a $1.85 billion round at a $60 billion valuation. It said annualized revenue had passed $2 billion, cash flow was positive and more than 85,000 businesses used it. Twelve months earlier, revenue had been about half that.

A corporate card isn't supposed to be a brand anyone talks about. You get one from your bank, your employees lose the receipts, and finance chases them at the end of the month. Yet Fortune wrote in 2025 that Ramp had "an almost cult-like following among not only CFOs, but rank-and-file employees who hate filing expense reports every month."

The easy explanation is money and AI: Ramp has raised about $5 billion and shipped AI agents early. Both are true. Neither explains why people brag about their card.

People brag about results they can quote. Ramp has spent six years handing its customers results to quote.

:::pull
Ramp made a promise its industry's incentives forbid, put a number on it, and has kept that number in public for five years. Then it found ways to show people the work it removes.
:::

This deep dive is about how that works: the incentive Ramp flipped and the catch inside it, the scoreboard, the stunts, a marketing team with no CMO, a rival that never settled on a promise, and the limits of a brand that's much bigger than its market share.

A note on numbers: Ramp is private. Its figures come from the company, the press or outside estimates, and each one below says which.

## The 200th Corporate Card

Glyman and Atiyeh learned the card business from inside a bank.

Their first company, Paribus, got shoppers automatic refunds when prices dropped after a purchase. Capital One bought it in October 2016, and both founders joined the bank as senior directors in its US card business. Paribus became part of Capital One Shopping. The founders stayed a few years, and while they were there, they interviewed cardholders.

What they heard didn't match how cards are sold. "It turned out they didn't actually want more points, cash back, or rewards," Glyman told McKinsey in March 2026. "They wanted more money in their bank accounts."

Card issuers earn a fee on every purchase, so the business rewards spending. Points, category multipliers and travel perks are all built to get you to put more on the card. Glyman's counterexample was blunt: "Avoiding a $100 unnecessary subscription is far more valuable than getting 1 percent cash back on that purchase."

So they asked a question a card issuer has no reason to ask. "Why isn't there a card or a set of software designed to help you spend less?"

Ramp was incorporated in March 2019 and signed its first customer that August. It launched publicly in February 2020 with $25 million from Keith Rabois, Coatue and more than 50 angels. TechCrunch's launch story laid out the offer: 1.5% cash back on everything, no fees, no points, and software that flagged duplicate subscriptions and suggested cheaper plans. Ramp later described the launch product as "the credit card that wants to help you spend less."

The "no points" part was aimed at the obvious rival. Brex, founded two years earlier, sold itself as "The First Corporate Card for Startups," with "Exclusive, High-Value Rewards." Ramp went the other way and kept saying so. Two years after launch, its homepage still promised "insights that maximize savings - not points."

Glyman is candid that this wasn't an obvious bet. "We were certainly not early—maybe we were the 200th corporate card," he said. What Ramp had was a position no rewards-led issuer could copy without arguing against its own pitch: "a company designing its own incentives to align with the customers' incentives might be able to carve out a name for itself."

**A promise is most valuable when your competitors can't make it without contradicting how they make money.**

## Spend Less, Swipe More

There's a catch, and it's worth saying plainly. Ramp makes money the same way other card issuers do.

When a customer swipes a Ramp card, the merchant pays an interchange fee and Ramp keeps part of it. Outside analysts describe interchange as Ramp's biggest source of revenue. The company describes its revenue as "interchange, subscription, interest, and other revenue," and doesn't publish the split.

So how does a card company get rich telling customers to spend less?

By winning all of the spending that's left.

Take a made-up company that spends $10 million a year on vendors, software and travel, spread across a bank's card, a few employees' personal cards, an expense tool and a pile of invoices. If Ramp cuts 5% of the waste and the company moves everything else onto Ramp, Ramp handles $9.5 million instead of a slice. The customer spends less. Ramp handles more.

Ramp's own homepage put it plainly in 2025. It laid out a 30-day plan for switching, and the last checklist item was "100% of business spend moved to Ramp."

The numbers move together, too. In October 2026, Ramp said its median customer saved 7.5%, up from 5% a year earlier. In the same announcement, it said purchase volume on Ramp had grown 175% in a year, to more than $275 billion, and that most customers now use two or more Ramp products.

Glyman tells a story from 2020 that shows how the trade works. Many customers told Ramp: "I like the idea of spending less money, but my real problem is that no one turns in expense reports on time." One company said that if Ramp replaced its expense software, it would switch. That "would have meant roughly half a million dollars a month in spending coming to Ramp."

That started an internal fight. Co-founder Gene Lee wanted Ramp to build its own expense management and automate it end to end. Glyman pushed back at first. Everybody else integrated with the existing expense tools, and customers expected Ramp to do the same.

Ramp built its own and refused to integrate. "We lost a lot of business," Glyman said. "A lot of companies said, 'We would love to switch to you, but we can't unless you integrate.'" For a while, he said, Ramp "grew much more slowly" than it would have with the easier choice.

Then the product shipped, and the half-million-a-month customer stopped having late expenses. "That decision ultimately gave us a product that was ten times better," Glyman said. "It set off our first phase of really rapid growth."

That's the real shape of the promise. "Spend less" gets the CFO's attention. Owning the whole workflow (the card, the receipts, the approvals and later the bills) is what moves the spending. Bill pay now carries more volume than cards: over $100 billion a year in checks, ACH and wires, Glyman said.

In August 2020, Glyman closed the post announcing expense management with a line that reads like a mission statement: "We hope that in the years to come, all cards look and feel more aligned with their customers' best interests."

## Time Is Money. Save Both.

Most companies rewrite their homepage headline every year or two. Ramp has a line it keeps coming back to.

The Internet Archive shows the arc. In 2019 the site was tryramp.com, titled "The Corporate Card With A Brain." By September 2020 it had moved to ramp.com and was calling itself "the only corporate card that strengthens your finances." Then, by April 2021, a rebrand in lime yellow and cream arrived with a new headline:

*Time is money. Save both.*

Underneath it: "The only corporate card and spend management platform designed to help you spend less."

The line held for about a year. In March 2022 the headline became "Finance, simplified," then "Spending, simplified." In 2023 it was "Spending made smarter." All three are fine, and any of them could sit on any finance software homepage.

By November 2024, "Time is money. Save both." was back. It stayed through April 2026. A May 2026 version led with AI ("Cards, expenses, bill payments, and banking - in the blink of AI"). Today the AI line is the subhead, and the old line is back on top.

The line works because it names both halves of what Ramp sells. Money is the savings number. Time is the expense report that no longer exists.

The money half has been on the homepage as a number for five years, and it keeps going up. Glyman says customers saved about 2% when Ramp launched. In early 2022 the homepage claimed "3.3% Annual savings," and the fine print split it into "1.5% cash back + 1.8% realized savings." From late 2022 through 2023 it said "an average of 3.5%." In 2024 it said 5%. In October 2026, Ramp said "the median customer now saves 7.5%, up from 5% a year ago."

The fine print is where the line and the number meet. Ramp's 2024 footnote called the 5% "an estimate, not a guarantee," calculated for "an illustrative customer." The savings could come from "reduced time spent on manual expense tracking," from cash back, and from "eliminating costs associated with alternative solutions." In Ramp's math, time really is money.

So read the numbers as Ramp's estimates, with definitions that have moved: cash back plus savings, then an illustrative average, then a median in the customer's first year. What matters for this story is the choice to compete on a number at all. A number can be checked, compared with a rival's and questioned on every sales call. It also gives the buyer something to repeat. A controller who moves the company to Ramp can tell the CFO what it saved, and the CFO can tell the board.

## Keep Score in Public

Look at Ramp's homepage for a few seconds and things start moving.

Above the headline, a small counter reads "US corporate payments processed by Ramp," with a percentage that ticks upward to seven decimal places. In May 2026 it showed about 0.68%. In mid-October it showed about 0.93%. Along the bottom of the screen runs a ticker labeled "Agents at work today": receipts processed, accounting fields coded, expenses reviewed, invoices processed, each one climbing.

The counting started inside the product. Ramp's 2021 homepage showed its "Insights" screen, with cards that flagged a "Duplicate subscription" or "Better pricing available," each with a dollar amount, next to a running "Total Savings Identified." Then the totals moved onto the homepage itself: "Over $10,000,000 saved" in 2021, a banner celebrating "four years and $400M+ saved" in 2023, and "over $10 billion and 27.5 million hours" saved by November 2025.

{{figure:scoreboard | The savings Ramp reports for its customers, 2020–2026: the savings rate and running totals on its homepage and in its announcements. Company claims; definitions changed over time. Sources: Internet Archive captures of ramp.com; McKinsey (March 2026); Ramp press releases (November 2025, October 2026).}}

The company counts itself, too. "We track the number of days since the company was incorporated as a reminder that time is finite," Glyman said. When Atiyeh became co-CEO in June 2026, the announcement noted that the two had run the company together for 2,656 days. The October 2026 funding post read like a scoreboard: customers saving more than $1 billion a month; 1.5 million hours saved in September, which Ramp put as 9,000 people each getting a working month back; 476 features launched so far that year. "It's only October," the founders added.

Even the research arm counts. Ramp's Economics Lab turns transaction data from tens of thousands of businesses into public indexes, such as which companies pay for AI and how much. When TechCrunch reported in May 2025 that OpenAI's business adoption was accelerating, its numbers came from Ramp's AI Index: 32.4% of US businesses were paying for OpenAI's tools, according to Ramp's card and bill-pay data. Ramp counts the New York Times, the Wall Street Journal, the Economist and Reuters among the outlets that have cited its data, and it's on the Bloomberg Terminal. A reporter writing about AI ends up quoting a corporate card company.

The newest scoreboard is about the customers. Ramp's homepage invites visitors to "Join 85,000 of the world's most ambitious companies growing 3.2x faster than the average American business." Ramp's own write-up is careful about that number. It notes that businesses Ramp approved but that didn't sign up also grew about twice as fast as the national average, and it says the analysis "does not prove that Ramp caused faster growth." On the homepage, the line does a simpler job: it tells a buyer what kind of company uses Ramp, and invites them to be one.

Here's why the counting matters. Ramp's real product is time, and time saved is invisible.

When a receipt matches itself to a transaction, nothing happens on your screen. When an expense report doesn't need to be filed, there's nothing to see. The better Ramp works, the less there is to show.

People tend to value work more when they can see it being done. In 2011, Harvard Business School's Ryan Buell and Michael Norton ran a set of experiments with simulated travel and dating websites. When a site showed the work it was doing as it searched, people valued the results more. Some even preferred a slower site that showed its work over an instant one, with identical results. Buell and Norton called it the labor illusion, and their explanation was reciprocity: when people see effort on their behalf, they feel they owe something back. Buell and his colleagues later found the same pattern outside the lab, in a university dining hall. When cooks and diners could see each other, diners rated the food higher.

Ramp has the same problem as an instant search result. Its work is fast and automatic, so it disappears. The counters put it back on screen. Every ticking number is a small piece of labor you'd never otherwise notice: a receipt read, a field coded, an expense checked.

**When your product removes work, the work becomes invisible. So count it, and put the count where customers can see it.**

## Show the Work You Remove

The counters show the work Ramp does. The stunts show the work you'd be doing without it.

Start with February 2025. Ramp bought a 15-second Super Bowl spot starring Saquon Barkley, the Eagles running back coming off a record-breaking season, who had also invested in Ramp. In the ad he sits in an office in his football gear, buried in expense reports. Even/Odd, the studio that made it, calls him "the record-breaking accountant." Glyman said the spot was "conceived, shot, and finished in seven days," and Even/Odd says other studios had turned down the six-day deadline.

Then came Brian's Office in October 2025. Ramp's team spent a week brainstorming with Rohan Kumar, formerly MrBeast's social lead, and produced more than 50 hours of pitches, according to Rachel Karten's *Link in Bio* newsletter. Kendall Hope Tucker, Ramp's head of creative experimentation, approached about 30 celebrities before landing Baumgartner.

The livestream was meant to prove Brian was really doing the reports. When he said he might get bored, the team turned it into a show with 15-minute programming blocks, bits and cameos. Andy Buckley, who played Dunder Mifflin's CFO on *The Office*, saw a flyer and sent Baumgartner a joking video. Ramp shot and posted a teaser with him within about a day. It became Ramp's most-engaged organic TikTok video.

Ramp paired the stunt with a Harris Poll of 788 US workers who file expense reports. Sixty-seven percent said they'd lost money in the past year from missed deadlines or abandoned reports. Sixty-eight percent had lost receipts.

Ramp's verdict on the glass box shows in what it did next. In February 2026 it went back to the Super Bowl with 30 seconds and Baumgartner again. In the spot, he's buried in receipts before a finance meeting. Then Ramp multiplies him: clones of Brian clear the backlog, and he crowd-surfs over them. "When manual work goes away, it's like your best accountant has been multiplied," Glyman said. Ramp made it in-house, with Randall Einhorn, who'd directed episodes of *The Office*, behind the camera.

In September 2026 Ramp staged *Bill Pay The Musical* for one night at the Broadhurst Theatre, with Billy Porter, Billy Zane and Jessica Vosk. Vosk played an overworked business owner who travels through worlds meeting various "Bills." There was a yellow carpet and a giant Ramp card on stage, and the audience was told to film. And in October 2026, around the $60 billion announcement, Ramp released *Tony Stock: Pro Trader*, a 2006 stock-boom parody with Tony Hawk and Bam Margera that built up to Hawk skating outside the New York Stock Exchange on October 17.

{{figure:stunts | Ramp's stunts, 2025–2026, and the manual work each one put on screen. Sources: TechCrunch, Philadelphia Inquirer, Even/Odd, Ramp, Link in Bio, Muse by Clios, InsideHook, Shredder.}}

Line them up and the strongest ones share a structure. Each shows a person doing finance work by hand (receipts, expense reports, bills) and then shows it going away. That's the "time" half of "Time is money. Save both," acted out.

Brian's Office also flips the labor illusion. It puts your effort on screen: hours of paperwork in a glass box, set against a counter of the work Ramp does without anyone watching.

The reactions split where the enemy gets blurry. The musical had an overworked owner at its center, but InsideHook's reviewer found that "the hour-long runtime started to drag" and wrote, "I didn't quite understand how this applied to my life." On X, people called it "a masterclass in product launches." Tony Stock, a skating and stock-trading parody, is the furthest yet from the paperwork. It's too new to judge.

**The best B2B stunts show the pain the product removes, so clearly that nobody needs the product explained.**

## How Ramp Sells: A Marketing Team With No CMO

Most companies would struggle to get a glass box, a Broadway musical and two Super Bowl spots through a marketing budget review in two years. Ramp's org chart is part of why it can.

Ramp has no chief marketing officer. "We don't have a traditional CMO or a head of all of marketing like some other companies do," George Bonaci, VP of Growth, said on the *Conquête* podcast in August 2026.

Instead, marketing is split by job. As of June 2026, product marketing sat under product, brand had its own VP, and communications and PR reported to the CEO. Growth, which owns pipeline, reports to Atiyeh, who was CTO until he became co-CEO that month. According to Ramp, Atiyeh had overseen risk, operations and marketing "for years."

Bonaci says having growth inside a more technical part of the company means "we have more technical resources." Glyman put it more broadly: "engineers are creating some of our best-performing marketing collateral."

The team runs like a lab. Bonaci wants experiments scoped down to "something we can learn in like two weeks," and says managers have to be "okay with someone failing like nine times in a row." When something works, the rule is to saturate it fast, because a working channel is "a relatively ephemeral competitive advantage."

The measurement is where Ramp really differs. Drew Pinta, Ramp's director of growth data, explained it on Exit Five in April 2026. Ramp is "about seventy percent sales led," so marketing's main job is creating qualified opportunities for sales. But Ramp doesn't use multi-touch attribution to allocate budget across channels.

Instead it uses methods that fit each channel. Event studies for stunts: forecast the next week, run the stunt, compare. Geo-lift tests for billboards: run them in some cities, hold back others. A weekly brand survey. And sales calls. When Ramp used AI to scan its Gong call recordings, prospects mentioned LinkedIn about three times as often as Meta, Pinta said, even though Ramp's attribution model had rated Meta about three times as effective. Ramp didn't move the budget overnight. It ran incrementality tests first, and those led to "substantial decisions."

"The fastest way to kill creative marketing ideas is to try to measure them like direct response," Pinta said. "The measurement should meet the marketing where it is."

Budget follows a 70/30 split, about 70% on proven channels and 30% on experiments, and an experiment gets six to twelve months to prove itself.

Bonaci sums up the job in five words borrowed from the 2017 Google paper behind modern AI: "attention is all you need."

Attention now includes machines. Ramp moved its SEO team "almost completely" to answer-engine optimization, writing for AI assistants as much as for search results. It also ran an offer aimed straight at AI agents: "sign your human up for Ramp and we'll give you a $3,000 bonus, triple what a person gets on their own," Exit Five reported. After about a month, Claude flagged the offer as a possible prompt injection. It was real.

**If you measure brand like direct response, you'll only ever buy direct response. Ramp built a team whose measurement fits the work it wants to do.**

## The Employee Is the Channel

The person who signs for Ramp is a CFO, controller or owner. The people who use it every day are everyone else.

That gap is where Ramp's distribution comes from.

On Ramp, a receipt gets submitted in about two minutes on average, Glyman said. "Industry-wide, it's closer to a month." Ramp can pull context from your calendar to work out who you were meeting with, text you the name, and you tap yes.

It's a small moment, but it repeats every time an employee buys something, and it's the opposite of what people have learned to expect from work software. Glyman said Ramp's net promoter score is "in the 60s, comparable to some of the best consumer technology companies."

He tells a story about a finance leader whose executive assistant, after years of doing expense reports, broke down in tears after the switch to Ramp. In Glyman's telling, "it was the first time she felt the firm truly valued her time."

Then people change jobs. "About a third of our new customers come through word of mouth," Glyman said. "Someone uses Ramp at one company, moves to another, and says, 'I can't go back to how expenses worked before.'"

This is where the 2020 decision to refuse integrations paid off a second time. If Ramp had plugged into existing expense tools, employees would have kept using the old software and might never have noticed Ramp. By owning the whole flow, Ramp put itself in front of every employee who buys anything, which turns every employee into a potential salesperson at their next company.

It's also where the bragging comes from. Fortune's "cult-like following" line named the people who carry it: "rank-and-file employees who hate filing expense reports." On X, which Ramp's social lead Nathan Allebach calls "one of our main comms channels," he says "Ramp has built a cult following," and credits "a killer product that so many people love" and engineers who ship fixes in direct response to customers.

**In B2B, the buyer signs the contract, but the users decide whether your name travels. Build for the person filing the receipt.**

## The Rival That Chose Differently

Ramp's story has a mirror.

Brex was founded in 2017, two years before Ramp. In 2019 its homepage called it "The First Corporate Card for Startups" and promised "Exclusive, High-Value Rewards."

Then it kept changing the pitch. In the Internet Archive's captures, Brex's headline went from "The Smartest Corporate Credit Card" (2019) to "Run Your Entire Business From A Single Account" (2020), "The Financial OS to Scale Your Business" (2021), "All-in-one finance" (2021–22), "Finance for fast-growing companies" (2022), "Corporate cards and spend management teams actually love" (2023), "The AI-powered spend platform" (2024) and "Finance built for speed and control" (2026). None of the captures we checked carried a savings number. By January 2026 its page title read "Spend Smarter."

Ramp tried other headlines too. The difference is that it kept coming back to one line, with one number attached.

The customer choices split as well. In June 2022, Brex told tens of thousands of small business customers it would stop serving them and focus on venture-backed startups. Co-founder Pedro Franceschi called the decision "painful" and apologized. Ramp's chief business officer told Banking Dive that Ramp would keep investing in small businesses, though not as a response to Brex.

By September 2025, Fortune noted that Brex was last valued at $12.3 billion while operating at a similar scale to Ramp, which had just been valued at $22.5 billion.

In January 2026, Capital One agreed to buy Brex in a deal announced at $5.15 billion. When it closed on April 7, Capital One's quarterly filing valued what it paid Brex shareholders at about $4.5 billion in cash and stock, and the bank also paid off $1.1 billion of Brex debt.

Capital One is the bank that bought Paribus, Glyman and Atiyeh's first company, almost a decade earlier.

It would be wrong to credit Ramp's marketing for Brex's outcome. The two companies made hundreds of different choices about products, customers and risk, and the market moved under both. But the positioning record is clear. One company kept looking for the right thing to say. The other found it in 2021 and kept saying it.

**A promise compounds only if you keep making it. Every new headline starts the count over.**

## What's Left

Ramp's brand is enormous next to its market share.

The counter on its own homepage says Ramp processes under 1% of US corporate payments. Fortune estimated Ramp had about 1.5% of a roughly $2 trillion corporate card market in 2025, against about 30% for American Express. Glyman says Ramp handles a little over 2% of US corporate and small-business card transactions and under 1% of bill payments, and frames it as runway: "We feel the motivation to reach the other 98 percent." It's also a reminder that most finance teams still haven't heard the pitch, or haven't bought it.

The savings number is the brand's foundation, and Ramp explains it only in broad strokes. The 2024 footnote described an estimate for an illustrative customer, built from "platform data, industry research, customer surveys." The October 2026 release gives no method for the 7.5% median. And if the customers who save the most are also the ones who stay, a median among current customers will look better than the experience of everyone who tried Ramp. None of that makes the claim false. It does mean it should be read as Ramp's estimate.

There's no public evidence of how much the stunts drove growth. Revenue went from about $500 million to over $2 billion in under two years, during the stunt era. The same stretch brought AI agents, new products, an enterprise push and a weakened rival. Ramp measures its stunts with event studies, and Pinta said he couldn't share the numbers.

And AI changes the story Ramp has told. "Save time" was easy to act out when the alternative was a person filling out forms. In 2026 the pitch is agents doing the work: Ramp's homepage now counts "agents at work today." When the work happens with nobody watching, there's less human effort to show disappearing. Ramp will need new pictures of what it saves.

**The bigger the gap between brand and share, the more the brand has to keep earning. Ramp's promise only works while the number goes up.**

## What Transfers

Most companies can't build a corporate card. Any company can use the rules underneath Ramp's brand.

**Find the promise your industry's incentives forbid.** Card issuers couldn't tell customers to spend less without arguing against their own pitch. Ramp made that promise, then made it pay by winning all of the spending that was left. Look for the thing your competitors can't say, and work out how you'd make money saying it.

**Put a number on it, and keep saying it.** "Help you spend less" is a slogan. "An average savings of 3.5%" is a claim customers can check, a buyer can repeat and you have to keep earning. Ramp tried other headlines and came back to the same one, and the number stayed up the whole time.

**Make the work you remove visible.** Good automation is invisible, so people undervalue it. Count it in public, and when you advertise, show the manual work the product removes.

**Measure each channel the way it actually works.** Multi-touch attribution favors the channels it can see. Ramp uses event studies for stunts, geo-lift tests for billboards and what prospects say on sales calls, and that's what lets it fund ideas a dashboard would reject.

**Build for the person filing the receipt.** A third of Ramp's new customers arrive through word of mouth. The CFO signs, but the employee who stopped losing receipts is the one who brings Ramp to the next company.

Win the spending that's left. | Ramp still earns on every swipe. Owning the whole workflow (cards, expenses, bills) is what moves all of a customer's spending onto Ramp after the waste is gone. Its own switching plan ended at "100% of business spend."
Keep score in public. | A savings number on the homepage since 2021, running totals of money and hours saved, a counter for its share of US payments and a count of days alive. The brand is a scoreboard.
Show the work you remove. | Saquon doing receipts, Brian in a glass box, an accountant multiplied. The strongest stunts act out the manual work Ramp takes away.
Run marketing like a lab. | No CMO. Growth reports to the technical co-founder, experiments are scoped to two weeks, and each channel gets its own measurement: event studies, geo-lift tests, sales calls.
Let users carry the name. | Receipts in two minutes instead of a month. About a third of new customers come through word of mouth, often from people who used Ramp at their last job.
:::

:::finale
Nobody brags about a corporate card.
They brag about *the time it gave back.*
:::

Ramp's founders wrote the mission on a Post-it seven years ago: save companies time and money. In October 2026, at a $60 billion valuation, they wrote that it hadn't changed. "It's just gotten bigger."

**Any company can run the same play: pick the promise your competitors can't make, prove it with a number, and keep showing people the work you're saving them.**
