On September 9, 2026, Clay announced a $115 million round at a $7.1 billion valuation. In the same announcement, it said it was putting $1 million into a scholarship fund to train GTM engineers.

That second line is the stranger one. Software companies usually pay to train their customers. Clay was paying to train a job title.

The title had taken off. By early 2026 there were about 3,000 open GTM engineer roles across the major job boards, according to two separate industry analyses. One of them, the State of GTM Engineering Report, analyzed 3,342 postings and found that 69% mentioned Clay by name.

Four and a half years earlier, almost nobody wanted what Clay was selling. The company was founded in 2017, and as of spring 2022 its revenue was "close to $0," co-founder and CEO Kareem Amin told First Round Review.

By December 2025, Clay said it had crossed $100 million in annual recurring revenue, about two years after crossing $1 million.

The obvious explanation is AI, and it explains a lot. Clay's research agent runs on large language models, and some of its best-known customers are the companies building those models.

But Clay's revenue grew roughly tenfold in 2022, before any of that, according to Contrary Research. And plenty of startups wired GPT into a sales tool without becoming the name in the job ad.

:::pull
What Clay did was find the people who already did growth work by hand, make them visibly better at it, and give them a name. Then it sold to the companies that hired them.
:::

This deep dive is about how that worked: the five years before it did, the agencies that got it first, the pricing that let it spread, the title that turned users into a labor market, and the parts of the business Clay still doesn't own.

A note on numbers: Clay is private. Its figures come from the company, the press, or outside estimates, and each one below says which.

## Five Years of the Wrong Customers

Amin and Nicolae Rusan had already built and sold a company before Clay. They met at McGill, left Microsoft in 2010, and launched Frame, a Shopify app for tablet storefronts. Sailthru bought it in May 2012, about five months after launch, according to Contrary Research.

After stints running product at The Wall Street Journal and Dow Jones, both left by the end of 2015. In 2017 they started Clay in Brooklyn.

The goal was big and vague. "We wanted to give the power of programming to more people," Amin told Brian Halligan on Sequoia's *Long Strange Trip* podcast.

The first idea was to rebuild the terminal, the command line developers type into. They dropped it because it would only help people who could already code.

The founders now describe the next step as a spreadsheet with APIs built in. The Internet Archive shows a longer road. In August 2017, clay.run sold "Cloud Hosted Microservices," a set of tools to "build, share and remix Functions As A Service." By 2019 the site promised to "connect apps and custom code to create automated workflows." Only in 2021 did it say: "This spreadsheet fills itself."

The customers were all over the place. Recruiters used it. Salespeople used it for inbound and outbound. Front-end engineers used it as a low-code backend.

One company used Clay to push data into NetSuite, its accounting system, heavily enough to cause server outages. "We had a customer, but it was the wrong customer," Amin said.

Co-founder Varun Anand, who joined as an advisor and became a co-founder in September 2021, remembers "maybe 10, 25 paying customers" at that stage, paying somewhere between $30 and $200 a month. Nearly all of them churned within a year, he told First Round.

Amin summarized the period for Halligan: "two years of us building, two years of us kind of being confused."

The fix wasn't a new idea. It was the decision to stop chasing the biggest version of the old one. Around three and a half years in, the team was mostly serving salespeople, but the website and product still spoke to everyone. So they cut the features that didn't serve sales, rewrote the marketing, and committed.

"Once we committed, it almost worked instantaneously," Amin said.

**Clay didn't find its market by having a better vision. It found it by noticing who was already using the product and saying yes to them.**

## The Only People Who Got It

Committing to sales still left a question: which salespeople?

Anand went looking. He interviewed about 30 people from Modern Sales Pros, an online community of sales leaders. Most of them didn't see the point. One group did: people who ran cold email agencies.

The fit was obvious once he saw it. An agency runs outbound for many clients at once, and every client needs the same thing: a list of the right companies, the right people at them, and something specific to say. Agency owners were technical enough to wire tools together, scrappy enough to try something rough, and price-sensitive enough to care what each lookup cost.

They also traveled in packs. Their WhatsApp groups, with names like SaaS Yacht Club and Sales Technicians, became Clay's main channel for finding problems to solve, Anand said.

In January 2022, Clay narrowed its focus to outbound data enrichment for growth and sales teams. It launched on Product Hunt the next month and put up a waitlist, which came and went until about July 2023. The homepage had a new line: "Create live lists of people, without a line of code."

Then came the sales motion that wasn't really sales. Anand ran what he called reverse demos. Instead of showing a prospect the product, he had them sign up, share their screen, and solve a real problem of their own while he guided them over Zoom. He did "eight of these calls a day."

Early on, closing a customer at $200 or $300 a month took about seven calls. By early 2023 it took one, or none. Every session ended the same way: the prospect joined Clay's Slack community, and Anand stayed on the call until they'd sent a message there.

That Slack was the only support channel Clay offered. It started with about 200 people. By mid-2024 it had more than 11,000, and a 2026 Clay job posting put it at 30,000.

Support in public did two things a help desk can't. Every answer helped the next person with the same question. And every expert who answered became visible to everyone else.

The agencies wanted that visibility. They posted about Clay on LinkedIn because they wanted to be seen as experts on the new tooling, Anand said. Their marketing was Clay's marketing.

Amin has said he knew Clay had product-market fit when users started calling themselves Clay experts and forming agencies, and when they took Clay with them to new jobs. Revenue grew about tenfold in 2022 and again in 2023, according to Contrary Research.

**Clay's first customers were also its first marketers, because using Clay well made them look good in public.**

## Not Another Data Provider

By March 2023, Clay's homepage had found its sharpest line: "Not another data provider. All of the data providers."

The established players sold their own databases. ZoomInfo and Apollo each built huge contact datasets and charged for access. Each was good in some places and thin in others.

Clay didn't try to build a better database. It plugged into dozens of them: more than 50 sources by its own count in early 2023, and more than 150 by 2026. Then it let users chain them together.

The best-known version is the waterfall. Ask for someone's work email, and Clay checks one provider. If that one comes back empty, it tries the next, and the next, until one finds it. A single vendor can't offer that, because a single vendor only has its own data.

Amin's view is that the market leaders weren't the best sources anyway. Many smaller providers have data that is "better and cheaper than the dominant kind of market players," he told Sequoia's *Training Data* podcast, but customers didn't know they existed.

The product was built around a spreadsheet. Each row is a company or a person. Each column is a lookup, a calculation, or a step: find the email, check the tech stack, score the account, write the first line. The team chose two principles and stuck to them: aggregate "every data source in the world," and be "the most flexible tool in the world." When flexibility and ease of use conflicted, flexibility won.

The pricing followed the product. "All the value is in the columns," Anand said, so the unit of pricing became the credit, spent each time a column ran. In April 2023, Clay's $349 Basic plan included 20,000 credits, and every call to an enrichment provider counted as one. Customers didn't need their own accounts or API keys for each provider; Clay bundled them.

That looked odd at the time. In 2022, nearly every sales tool charged per seat, and customers were "shocked" that Clay didn't, Anand said. Clay didn't even have billing inside the product until it passed $1 million in ARR. Before that, Anand sent invoices through Stripe by hand.

The 2023 plans still capped seats, at two, five and ten users. By May 2024, the pricing page said "Unlimited users." From then on, adding a colleague cost nothing. Only usage did.

The flexibility had a cost: Clay was hard to learn. It also had a payoff that is easy to underrate.

People value what they build themselves. In a set of studies published in 2012, Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely had people assemble simple IKEA storage boxes. The builders bid about 63% more for their box than people who were handed an identical one already built. But the effect faded when people couldn't finish the build (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 2012).

A Clay table works the same way. Someone who builds a working outbound machine column by column values it far more than a list someone handed them. That only holds if they finish, which is what the reverse demos, the public Slack and the agencies were for.

**Clay sold power instead of simplicity, then surrounded the power with people who made sure users finished what they built.**

## The Spreadsheet Learns to Read

The next shift gave every column a new kind of power.

By March 2023, Clay's homepage advertised using GPT-3 "to create personalized emails." Later that year it introduced Claygent, an AI agent built on GPT-4 that could read the web on a user's behalf. Early users had to plug in their own OpenAI key.

Claygent turned questions that used to need a human into a column. Does this company's website mention SOC 2? Is it B2B? Where are its franchise locations? An agent visited the site, found the answer and wrote it into the row. To keep it affordable, Clay first asked GPT-4 which part of a page was most likely to hold the answer, then read only that part, according to OpenAI's case study.

The customer examples got stranger and more specific. Intercom used Claygent to find that companies mentioning fertility on their websites tended to need better customer support, according to Contrary Research. By mid-2024, about 30% of Clay's customers were using Claygent, and it passed a billion runs in June 2025.

Large language models also brought Clay its best-known customers. Anthropic used Clay to automate its inbound pipeline, and "we've tripled kind of the data coverage that they have at a fraction of the cost," Amin said. OpenAI doubled its enrichment coverage from 40% to 80%, according to Contrary Research. Clay says it was the only go-to-market company on Forbes' 2025 AI 50 list. By September 2026, it said 80% of the companies on that list were customers.

It's tempting to make AI the whole story. The timeline doesn't allow it. Clay's growth started in 2022, before Claygent existed, with agencies running waterfalls in a spreadsheet. What AI changed was the size of the opportunity. Every column that used to fetch a fact could now do a researcher's job.

**The AI wave gave Clay a better product and its best customers at the same time. But the people who knew how to use it were already there.**

## Name the Person, Not the Product

Clay didn't invent the words "GTM engineer." In July 2021, Scale AI posted a "GTM Engineer" opening on Hacker News, for an engineer to work closely with its go-to-market teams.

What Clay did was turn the words into a title, a definition and a career.

The story starts inside Clay. Its own sales team didn't fit the usual boxes. The people closing deals were also building the automations, running technical calls and writing the data workflows. Clay merged the jobs of sales development rep, account executive and sales engineer into one role, a team of about 12 people by Anand's count. Hiring ran through work trials. Candidates for sales roles were asked to build something creative in Clay. One built a table for picking Santa gifts; another built a tarot reading generator.

In 2023, Amin and Anand gave the role a name. "Let's call our own team go-to-market engineers," Amin recalled telling Halligan. An early investor pushed them to "own a title."

Amin is careful about what that means. "I don't think you can create necessarily a category," he said. "You can take something that is happening and name it."

The thing that was happening was real. Across startups, people in revenue operations, growth and sales ops had started automating their own work with code and AI. The name gave them an identity, and a better one than the jobs they'd come from. "We thought about it as a title," Amin said, "and I knew that one day it would be the category."

Then Clay built everything a profession needs. Clay University teaches the tool. Live cohorts and certifications credential the people who learn it. A creators program supports LinkedIn voices who already post about Clay. An experts program sends business to agencies, with a 20% revenue share on shared customers. Clay Clubs, local meetups run by volunteers, grew to about 70 by December 2025, the company said. In April 2026, Clay published a guide to building GTM engineering teams. In September came the scholarship.

The title spread to the customers. Clay's guide describes GTM engineering groups at Anthropic, Canva, Intercom, Notion, Ramp, Rippling and Verkada.

Job postings grew 205% from 2024 to 2025 and reached about 3,000 open roles in early 2026, according to the State of GTM Engineering Report. Two independent reviews of those postings found that 66% and 69% named Clay. The profession is still small. About 1,200 people held the title on LinkedIn in May 2026, according to an analysis by the sales tech company OnFire. And it skews senior: practitioners had a median of 7.4 years of experience before taking the title, according to RevGuild.

This is where the machine closes. A company hires a GTM engineer. The job ad asks for Clay. The person they hire already knows it, and often already loves it, because they built their career on it. Clay gets a champion inside the account before anyone signs a contract.

**A product category asks a buyer to change their mind. A job title asks them to update their résumé, and the résumé comes with the tool.**

## The Enterprise Came Through Its Builders

Clay wanted to stay self-serve as long as it could. Early deals were too small to pay for salespeople, Anand said. A sales team needs contracts of roughly $5,000 to $10,000 a year to make sense.

The first sign that large companies would come anyway was Rippling. The HR software company signed up in March 2023 and went from a few hundred dollars a month to several thousand within about eight weeks, with basic support.

Selling to the enterprise on purpose took longer, and the first two attempts were mistakes.

In fall 2023, Clay tried professional services. Two customers paid about $84,000 and $60,000, but the work was heavy, and it competed with the agencies Clay depended on. Early 2024 brought a platform fee on top of credits. Procurement teams pushed back on the fee, and Clay closed one or two deals a quarter.

What worked was selling into a budget that already existed. Instead of pitching Clay as a way to cut sales development or ops headcount, which buyers distrusted, the team led with data enrichment, a line item companies already paid for. It backed the pitch with a proof of concept that put a number on the result. In fall 2024, Clay dropped the separate platform fee and folded it into credits, borrowing from the way Snowflake prices.

The customers piled up. Clay said it had more than 2,500 when it raised a $46 million Series B led by Meritech in June 2024, at a $500 million valuation. Seven months later, a $40 million extension valued it at $1.25 billion, and it reported more than 5,000 customers. Revenue grew about sixfold in 2024 to roughly $30 million, according to Contrary Research and Sacra.

Inside large accounts, usage pricing without seat limits let Clay spread. Enterprise customers "double their activity with Clay on average within the first 12 months," the company wrote in 2026. When it announced $100 million in ARR in December 2025, it said enterprise net revenue retention was over 200% and that it had "never churned an enterprise customer."

And the people selling Clay to those enterprises were GTM engineers themselves. "Clay uses GTMEs to sell Clay," the company's guide says.

**Clay's enterprise sale didn't start with a salesperson. It started with a builder inside the account who already knew the tool.**

## Growth Without Buying Attention

Most fast-growing software companies buy their attention. Clay mostly earned it.

Clay's top-of-funnel playbook, as its head of growth marketing, Bruno Estrella, described it to the B2B marketing community Exit Five, ran on creators, partners and its own users rather than paid ads. It recruited creators from people who were already posting about Clay. "Authenticity was the filter," Exit Five's summary put it. Estrella's view was that the quality of engagement matters far more than follower count.

Search worked the same way. Clay built free tools, like email finders and calculators, and programmatic pages that matched what its audience searched for. "Their best SEO assets are interactive tools, not articles," Exit Five summarized.

The brand was unusual for B2B software from the start. Clay employed a full-time Claymation artist and once ran a spa day at Dreamforce, Anand said. Its own team handled the creative work.

The efficiency shows up in the company's own claims. "Every dollar we invest grows Clay roughly 15x," Amin and Anand wrote in December 2025. Clay was cash-flow positive for parts of 2025, earning more in interest than it burned, and hadn't touched the cash from its last raise, Amin told Crunchbase News in February 2026. The company had about 300 employees, up from 80 to 90 a year earlier.

Investors kept repricing it. A Sequoia-led tender valued Clay at $1.5 billion in May 2025. CapitalG led a $100 million round at $3.1 billion that August. A January 2026 employee tender put it at $5 billion. By the September Series D, Amin told The New York Times that annualized revenue was on track to reach about $200 million by the end of the quarter.

Compare that with the company Clay was positioned against. ZoomInfo's market value had fallen about 97% from its 2021 peak, and its net revenue retention was around 90%, according to Sacra.

**The cheapest way to reach your market is to make your users so good at their jobs that they want to talk about how they do it.**

## What Clay Doesn't Own

In March 2026, Clay changed its pricing and published the internal memo explaining why. It's an unusually candid document.

"Pricing primarily per data point made sense when data was the product," it says. But "customers don't come to Clay to buy inputs. They come to run outbound, score leads, and automate their GTM."

The memo also admits what had gone wrong. Clay had mispriced one of its plans in 2022, and that segment had run at a loss for years. The smallest self-serve customers paid close to 8 cents a credit while the largest paid about 80% less. And charging a fixed price for AI created adverse selection: customers with cheap AI tasks switched to their own API keys, while the expensive ones stayed on Clay.

So Clay split its pricing in two. Data Credits pay for the data, and Clay cut those prices by 50% to 90%. Actions pay for the work Clay's platform does: running enrichments, AI tasks and API calls, and pushing data to other tools. The entry plan became $185 a month, and the main team plan $495. Complex AI models are now billed by usage with "0% markup."

The memo calls the change "intentionally revenue- and profit-negative in the near term." Amin told Halligan the backlash was "minimal, or at least we perceived it to be minimal," with sentiment around 70% neutral. Competitors told a different story to agencies, framing the new plans as a price increase for heavy users.

The reason for the change is the most important thing in the memo. Clay was moving its business away from the part it doesn't own.

The data in a Clay waterfall belongs to other companies. That matters three ways.

The first is margins. Clay doesn't disclose its gross margin, but the research firm Sacra says its margins have historically looked "more like a data-heavy SaaS firm" than pure software, because credits pass through the cost of data and AI.

The second is access. In 2025, LinkedIn sued Proxycurl, a company that sold data scraped from LinkedIn profiles. Proxycurl shut down that July under a permanent injunction. There's no evidence Clay was affected, but the case showed that the raw material for prospecting tools can disappear by court order.

The third is imitation. Apollo launched its own waterfall in October 2025 and made it the default on paid plans that December. ZoomInfo built one into a product called GTM Studio, and its comparison pages pitch against Clay by pointing out that Clay has no database of its own.

**The waterfall was Clay's wedge. It was never going to be the moat. The moat is the people who know how to build with it.**

## When the Builder Is an Agent

That raises the question the whole machine depends on.

Clay's distribution is a profession of builders. They learned the tool, built careers on it, and brought it to their next employer. But the work they do is changing fast. An August 2026 review of GTM engineer postings found that 95% asked for AI or LLM skills. Only 66% named Clay.

If AI agents can build the workflows themselves, the profession that carried Clay may not need Clay to do its job.

Clay is betting the other way. Its homepage now features Sculptor, which builds workflows from plain-language instructions. There's a version of Clay inside ChatGPT. The Series D announcement described the goal as "a self-learning revenue engine," growth agents that find customers, watch for signals and launch outreach, in workflows people can inspect and adjust.

"We want to be the system of action," Amin told Sequoia's Alfred Lin. The data can live anywhere: in the CRM, in product analytics, with outside providers. "How you act on the data is going to be in Clay."

There's a fitting irony in where Amin explained the naming strategy. His interviewer, Brian Halligan, co-founded HubSpot and helped popularize "inbound marketing," a term that turned a way of working into a category HubSpot built its business on.

Clay's version is a step further. HubSpot named a practice. Clay named the practitioners.

**Every company that names a profession eventually has to earn its place in it again, because the profession doesn't belong to the company that named it.**

## What Transfers

Strip away the AI, the funding and the valuation, and Clay's growth comes down to one rule.

**Make your users visibly better at their jobs, and they'll become your distribution.**

Clay earned that in a specific order, and most of it applies well outside sales tools.

:::machine The way Clay *sells*
Find the people already doing the job by hand. | Clay's breakthrough customers weren't the biggest companies. They were cold email agencies with acute, repeated pain and the skills to use a rough product. Thirty interviews found them. Five years of building for everyone didn't.
Sell power to your first users, then help them finish. | A flexible product creates loyalty only when people complete what they build. Reverse demos, a public Slack and a network of agencies made sure they did.
Price so the product can spread. | Credits instead of seats, and no seat caps after 2024, meant one builder could pull in the whole team. Enterprise retention above 200% followed.
Name the person, not the product. | A category asks buyers to adopt your framing. A job title gives people a better version of their own career, and the tool comes with it. Two-thirds of the job ads for that title now name Clay.
Let the enterprise arrive through the people it hires. | Clay's two attempts at a conventional enterprise sale, services and platform fees, both stalled. What worked was the builder already inside the account, plus a pitch aimed at an existing budget line.
Know which layer you own. | The data was never Clay's, and the 2026 repricing was Clay paying to move its business onto the layer it does own.
:::

Some of it doesn't transfer. Clay found its market just as large language models arrived, and the companies building those models became its customers. Its founders survived five years of the wrong customers, with investors patient enough to wait. And its users happened to be people whose jobs involve being seen on LinkedIn.

The machine also has limits built in. The data belongs to someone else. The wedge has been copied. And the profession that carries Clay could learn to do the work without it.

For now, though, Clay is still running the play that turned it around. In September 2026, it put $1 million into training the next generation of GTM engineers.

It's paying to create the people who will buy it.
