In July 2026, Harvey posted a three-minute video shot in a wood-paneled library, with a stone fireplace, mustard velvet chairs and warm, low light.

The host was Gabriel Macht, the actor who played Harvey Specter on *Suits*, the lawyer the company is named after. Macht's guest was David Wakeling, the A&O Shearman partner who led the firm's early bet on Harvey. Harvey's caption did the bragging. "A&O Shearman was the first law firm to pilot Harvey in late 2022."

Wakeling told the story of the first demo. "I went to the senior partner of the firm, I slid an iPad across the table and I said, 'Ask any question you want,'" Wakeling recalled. "He thought back to his career and he asked a couple questions, and he's like, 'This is astonishing. We need to buy them.'" A&O didn't buy Harvey. It became Harvey's first law firm partner instead.

It's the most-viewed video on Harvey's X account, with more than a million views. Winston Weinberg, Harvey's CEO, shared it with a note. "David and the team at A&O Shearman bet big on Harvey when few law firm partners had ever heard of AI. At the time, Harvey was just a handful of engineers and lawyers living and working together in an Airbnb."

Lawyers are supposed to be the last people to buy new technology. They're paid to avoid risk, they bill by the hour, and every choice they make has to survive a partner, a client and sometimes a judge.

In 2023, generative AI was the riskiest thing a law firm could buy. It invented case law with total confidence. It needed client documents that firms are bound to protect. And it promised to do in minutes the work firms bill by the hour.

Yet four years after a first-year associate tested GPT-3 on landlord-tenant questions, Harvey is worth $15.5 billion. Its annual recurring revenue passed $400 million this fall. By its own count, 80% of the Am Law 100, the country's 100 highest-grossing law firms, use it.

The usual explanation is timing and money. Harvey had OpenAI's backing and early access to GPT-4, and it has raised more than $1.5 billion. Both are true, and neither explains why the most cautious buyers in business went first.

In professional services, a decision is only as safe as the people who made it before you. Harvey's founders understood that early. They got one elite firm to go first in public, made its choice easy to copy and kept a running count of everyone who did. Within three years, Harvey had gone from a risk a partner had to justify to something firms announced.

## The Riskiest Purchase in Law

Weinberg and Pereyra came at law from opposite ends.

Weinberg was a first-year litigation associate at O'Melveny & Myers in Los Angeles. Pereyra was a research scientist who had worked at DeepMind and Meta. They were roommates, and in 2022 Pereyra showed Weinberg what OpenAI's GPT-3 could do.

They ran a test a lawyer would trust. They wrote a step-by-step prompt grounded in California's landlord-tenant statutes and ran it on 100 real questions people had posted to Reddit's r/legaladvice. Three attorneys reviewed the answers. On 86 of the 100, at least two of the three said they'd send the answer without changing a word, by Weinberg's account to TechCrunch.

Then they cold-emailed OpenAI. "We cold emailed the general counsel of OpenAI," Weinberg said on Sequoia's *Training Data* podcast. Jason Kwon, Weinberg recalled, "basically responded, 'Oh my God, I had no idea these models were this good at legal.'" A call with OpenAI's leadership followed on July 4, 2022. That November, Harvey came out of stealth with $5 million led by the OpenAI Startup Fund.

That gave Harvey a famous backer. A law firm still had no reason to buy it.

Law firms are built to keep this kind of risk out. Work moves up a chain, from a junior associate's first draft to a senior associate's review to a partner's sign-off, and every word gets read. "Lawyers are argumentative, like, very argumentative," Weinberg said. "And I mean that in the best way." An hour of a US lawyer's time costs $352 on average, by Weinberg's figure, and a firm's reputation rides on every one of those hours.

Generative AI put all of that at risk, down to how firms get paid. Pereyra posted about that tension in October 2022, before Harvey had launched. There was "definitely a weird tension selling to lawyers given the billable hour," Pereyra wrote, "but so far the reception has been super positive."

So Harvey's real problem was who a cautious partner could point to if something went wrong.

**The riskier a purchase feels, the more the buyer needs someone else to have made it first.**

## One Firm Goes First

The first firm to take the risk was Allen & Overy, one of London's Magic Circle firms.

David Wakeling, who ran A&O's Markets Innovation Group, first came across Harvey in September 2022, Wired reported. A small trial began in November. It spread fast. By the end of it, about 3,500 of the firm's lawyers in 43 offices had asked Harvey around 40,000 questions, in 50 languages and 250 practice areas. "It's almost a fourth of the firm daily," Pereyra said.

Weinberg later credited Sarah Guo, an early investor, with "the intro that became our first customer."

On February 15, 2023, A&O announced the deal itself. "We are proud to be the first law firm to partner with Harvey, a ground-breaking generative AI platform that is designed to tackle legal problems," the firm posted.

That sentence did more for Harvey than any sales deck. A Magic Circle firm had put its name on the risk, in public, and treated going first as an honor. Reuters, Wired and the legal press picked it up. Wakeling called it "the beginning of a paradigm shift." Harvey's founders told LawSites that other firms were preparing to deploy it and that some "might also follow A&O's path."

A month later, PwC followed, with what it called "exclusive access among the Big 4" for its legal business, a network of more than 4,000 legal professionals, on an initial 12-month contract. In April, Pereyra described Harvey as "the first company built on @OpenAI's GPT-4," partnering "with elite professional service providers (like @PwC and @AllenOvery)."

The first firm also showed everyone else how to manage the risk. A&O kept a lawyer on every output and set modest expectations, "a couple hours a week-plus" saved, Wakeling told Reuters. Its internal description stuck. "In our internal communications, we refer to Harvey, our AI, as a very intelligent, very articulate 13yo," Wakeling told a New Scientist event in September 2023. "It doesn't know what it doesn't know."

That framing asked partners to do something they already knew how to do, which is supervise a gifted junior.

**A first customer who announces the deal as an honor gives every later buyer someone to point to. Make that announcement easy for them to make.**

## Elite by Design

Once Harvey had its first logos, it sold membership.

Its homepage in 2023 was a black page with a glowing blue wordmark and three words of pitch, "Unprecedented legal AI." The only button said "Join the Waitlist." The page title that browsers showed was blunter, "Generative AI for Elite Law Firms," and it was still there in March 2024. When Harvey raised its Series A from Sequoia in April 2023, LawSites noted that the product wasn't commercially available "except by invitation."

Scarcity did what it usually does. A knowledge management and legal tech lead at a European firm called Harvey's waitlist "FOMO at its best." The founders rarely gave interviews. Firms that got in could say they had.

The name pointed the same way. Harvey is named after Harvey Specter, the star lawyer of the TV series *Suits*. Weinberg told Non-Billable that a human name had a practical side, since "people prompt better when they act more human," and that it echoed "Harvard," which mattered for a premium feel. Harvey's own description of Specter, years later, was "an elite attorney."

## Demo the Buyer's Own Work

Getting a partner to look was one problem. Getting a partner to believe was another.

Weinberg's answer was to make every demo personal. "I don't think there is any excuse for someone who is building an AI product and trying to sell to not do hyper-personalized demos," Weinberg told Sequoia. "Whenever I would demo to a partner, I would try to use something that they recently worked on."

For litigators, that meant going to PACER, the federal courts' public filing system, finding a brief the partner had written and having Harvey draft the other side's response. In a 2025 Reddit AMA, the founders said it worked "super well" with litigators. It worked less well with deal lawyers, whose work lives in EDGAR filings.

Then came the invitation to argue. "I would sometimes say, you know, 'Was this a good argument, and how would you improve it?'" Weinberg said. "And if they were really bored on the demo and then you say that, they are reading every single word that comes out of Harvey, like every single word."

A demo on someone else's contract asks a partner to imagine. A demo on their own brief asks them to judge, which is what they're trained to do. And for many older partners, Weinberg said, Harvey was "their first AI product that they've used."

Law firms also turned out to be a forgiving first market. "A junior associate, if they get a task, they do the first draft of it, and then a second year reviews that, and then a fifth year might review that and then the partner reviews that and it goes out," Weinberg explained. So "the minimal viable quality of your output can be lower for a law firm than it can be for an in-house team." Harvey started where the review chain would catch its mistakes.

The people giving those demos were often lawyers themselves. "About 20% of our team are lawyers," Pereyra said in December 2025, and by June 2026 Harvey employed more than 200 former lawyers, Sourcery reported. Its "legal engineers" work alongside customers to build their workflows. "Every @Harvey all-hands begins with a customer interview," a16z, one of its investors, wrote in September 2026.

**A demo on the buyer's own work answers the only question a skeptic has, which is whether it works for them.**

## Firms Follow Firms

Weinberg has described the whole strategy in one answer. "The reason prestige is so important is because trust is the most important thing in professional services, right?" Weinberg told Sequoia in March 2025. "And so the reason we went after the larger firms is if you earn the trust of a few of those firms, the rest of them will trust you, and the rest of the firms downstream will definitely trust you, right?"

That's what happened, one market at a time.

In June 2024, Ashurst said it was "the only global law firm so far to roll-out Harvey across all offices globally from day one." In July 2025, Gowling WLG became the "first Canadian law firm to roll out Harvey AI enterprise-wide." The next month, Nigeria's Olaniwun Ajayi said it was "the first West African law firm" to adopt it. In January 2026, Harneys announced that it was "the first offshore law firm to deploy Harvey across all of its offices globally for all employees." In March, McCann FitzGerald became the first Irish firm to roll Harvey out firmwide, under the headline "Leads Irish Legal Market." In April, Mamo TCV became "the first law firm in Malta to adopt Harvey." And in October, Ogier, one of Harneys' offshore rivals, adopted it firmwide too.

Read in order, those announcements describe a prize. Each firm took a risk that had already been taken, then claimed the part that was still available, which was being first in its own market.

Every one of those figures is Harvey's own. But for a partner weighing the decision, the count does its job. It says the choice has already been made, many times, by firms they compete with.

An investor who studied lawyers using both Harvey and its main rival put it plainly in December 2025. "In conservative, status-driven markets like law, adoption is driven by customer psychology," wrote Wilson Kyi of Basis Set. "Buyers care about peer validation, reputation, and whether a decision will age well."

People lean on that kind of signal whenever quality is hard to judge. In 2006, the researchers Matthew Salganik, Peter Sheridan Dodds and Duncan Watts built an online music market and had 14,341 people listen to and download 48 songs by unknown bands. Some participants saw only the songs. The rest were sorted into eight separate "worlds," where they could also see how many times earlier visitors in their world had downloaded each song. Seeing other people's choices made the hits bigger and the outcomes harder to predict. "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well," the researchers wrote in *Science*, "but any other result was possible."

Legal AI in 2023 was a market where nobody could judge quality yet. So firms judged it the way those listeners did, by who had already chosen it.

In 2026, Harvey built the comparison into the product. Command Center, announced in May at Harvey's own conference in New York, shows a firm's leaders how their lawyers use Harvey and lets them compare their adoption against anonymized data from "more than 1,500 Harvey deployments globally." A recommendations panel surfaces features "that peer organizations have already enabled." The product shot on Harvey's homepage today is a Command Center dashboard with a toggle labeled "Peers."

**Each firm's yes made the next firm's yes easier to defend. After enough of them, holding out became the risk.**

## Then the Clients Asked

Weinberg's answer to Sequoia had a second half. "And their clients will trust you, right?"

Law firms answer to their clients, and by 2025 the clients were asking about AI. An innovation director at a large firm told Sacra, a research firm, that Harvey had become the tool clients requested by name. "Clients are pushing us toward Harvey because it's been more broadly promoted in the news," the director said, "and that has led clients to specifically request that we use it." And "partners also talk to their counterparts at other firms."

When Slaughter and May, another Magic Circle firm, adopted Harvey firmwide in April 2026, Harvey said the firm chose it partly for its "presence across the firm's client base."

Spellbook's CEO, Scott Stevenson, whose company competes with Harvey, put it less kindly in February 2026. In big firms, Stevenson wrote, "there is no incentive to reduce billable hours with AI hidden behind the curtain. AI needs to be a PR play." Stevenson meant it as a criticism. It also describes the shift. By 2026, a firm's AI was something it showed its clients.

Harvey built for that. Firms started making tools on Harvey and selling them to their own clients. "A&O Shearman is providing custom workflows built with Harvey to clients on a SaaS subscription model, splitting revenue with Harvey," Pereyra wrote in July 2025. Weinberg told TechCrunch that Latham & Watkins introduced Harvey to its clients. In December 2025, Harvey launched Shared Spaces, which lets a law firm host its clients inside Harvey as guests, with no Harvey subscription of their own.

Each of those put Harvey in front of a general counsel, introduced by a firm they already trusted. And the general counsels started buying.

In-house legal teams made up about 4% of Harvey's revenue at the start of 2025, Weinberg told TechCrunch. By November it was about a third. By June 2026 it was 42%, Weinberg told Sourcery. HSBC came on as a customer in January 2026. In March, Harvey's first in-house advisory board brought together legal leaders from HSBC, NBCUniversal, dentsu, Koch, MongoDB and Bridgewater Associates. By October, Harvey said 20% of the Fortune 500 and 40% of the Fortune 50 used it.

## Buying a Name

In 2026, Harvey started spending like a consumer brand.

It began with the name. In February, Harvey announced a brand partnership with Gabriel Macht, who played Harvey Specter for all nine seasons of *Suits*. "His portrayal of an elite attorney inspired a generation to pursue law," Harvey wrote. Weinberg told Non-Billable that the founders had talked about a partnership like this since Harvey's earliest days, and gave a plain reason. "Brand matters much more than most people think and we are committed to investing in it," Weinberg said. In July, Macht began interviewing Harvey's CEO and customers on camera. "We named @harvey after Harvey Specter," Weinberg posted. "Went full circle and finally sat down with @GabrielMacht."

Then came the sports deals. The first was a multiyear partnership with the US Open in March, "our first sports sponsorship - and a new arena for the Harvey brand." Within four months, Harvey had added the Dallas Mavericks and the Texas Rangers, Fulham in London, the Chicago Cubs, Paris Saint-Germain, VfB Stuttgart and the Toronto Maple Leafs.

Each deal came with the same explanation. Fulham, because "London is one of the world's leading legal markets." The Cubs, because "Chicago is one of the most important legal markets in the US." The Rangers, because Texas is "one of the largest legal markets in the U.S." Each one sat in a market where Harvey was opening or growing an office.

The look matched the money. The interview sets were wood-paneled libraries with lamplight and velvet chairs. In September, Harvey ran its first TV commercial, "Agreements make history," on ESPN, NBC, Amazon, Netflix and HBO Max. In October it became the "exclusive legal AI sponsor" of Sourcery, a tech business podcast.

Legal insiders weren't sure what it was for. "The hottest new thing in #legaltech isn't the tech, it's the sports sponsorships," Bob Ambrogi of LawSites posted in April, noting that Clio, Filevine and 8am had signed sports deals too. Legora, meanwhile, signed Jude Law for a campaign called "Law just got more attractive."

Here's our read. The audience is everyone a partner answers to when buying AI, from the general counsel watching the Cubs to the client who saw the commercial to the junior associate who grew up on *Suits*. A cautious buyer needs a name those people already know, so the purchase explains itself before anyone asks.

**When a buyer has to defend a choice, the brand is part of the product. Harvey spent on the part partners have to say out loud.**

## When the Product Can Be Copied

The spending came as Harvey's product got easier to approximate.

In February 2026, Flexport's CEO, Ryan Petersen, posted that "Claude for legal works seems to work just well as Harvey." A day later, the lawyer Zack Shapiro published "The Claude-Native Law Firm," arguing that for small firms a well-configured general-purpose AI beats specialized legal tools. It drew more than 8 million views. In April, Microsoft put a legal agent into Word. The same week, a former Latham lawyer posted, "I built their entire web application in two weeks and I'm making it open-source and free for everyone to use." In May, Kirkland & Ellis, the world's highest-grossing law firm, set aside $500 million to build its own AI platform, the Financial Times reported. In September, OpenAI launched Astra for Law.

Harvey's CEO saw where the real threat was. "It is a race against the labs," Weinberg told Sourcery in June. "Every single company on Earth is competing against them."

The same models also got expensive to resell. After Harvey upgraded its AI agents in March, customers used them so heavily that gross margin fell from "about 50% at the beginning of the year to -50% by June," Bloomberg reported, citing a person familiar with the matter. Harvey's token use rose twentyfold in 2026, the company told Bloomberg.

Harvey's answer was to own more of the stack. In 2024 it trained a custom legal model with OpenAI. In 2025 it went multi-model, adding Anthropic and Google. In August 2026 it released Tenet, its first model post-trained for legal work, built on Kimi K3, an open-weight model from China's Moonshot AI, and trained with Fireworks AI. Harvey said Tenet runs "at less than a fourth the cost of leading foundation models." The Chinese base drew questions from security-minded commentators, and praise from the investor and White House adviser David Sacks, who called Harvey "a great example of how American companies are building world-class specialized models." "We improved our gross margins from -50% to positive in a single quarter despite usage doubling month over month," Pereyra posted.

It also set the yardstick. In May 2026, Harvey open-sourced LAB, a benchmark of more than 1,200 legal tasks graded against more than 75,000 criteria written by lawyers. By July, Pereyra was congratulating xAI and Meta on models "achieving SOTA on @harvey's LAB." A company that rents much of its intelligence from the frontier labs was now grading them on legal work.

**When anyone can rent the same models, the moat is the trust you've already earned and the standards you get to set.**

## What's Left

Harvey's scoreboard is its own. Every Am Law figure in this piece comes from Harvey or its investors, and large firms often license more than one tool. The innovation director who told Sacra about client pressure also said the firm had Harvey licenses "and negotiated deals in place with Legora." Legora passed $200 million in annual recurring revenue in September 2026, by its CEO's count, and says it works with more than 2,300 customers. In October, Hogan Lovells chose Legora for a global rollout.

Usage is the most contested number. In May 2026, Weinberg posted that Harvey had "crossed 50% DAU/MAU" and that "more than half of our customers use Harvey every day." Critics pointed out that a ratio of daily to monthly users doesn't measure how many customers use Harvey daily. "…that's not what that means," replied Gordon Cassie, who founded the legal software company eno, in a post seen more than 330,000 times. In September 2025, an anonymous Reddit post that claimed to come from a former employee alleged low usage. Harvey answered by publishing a 77% seat utilization rate, 98% gross revenue retention and 167% net dollar retention.

The economics are still settling. Harvey says its margins recovered, but it hasn't published them. And its new model sits on a Chinese base that some firms will have to clear with their security teams.

Status also invites scrutiny. This fall, a federal judge in Manhattan declined to sanction lawyers over citation errors in a filing their firm had prepared with Harvey's tools. Judge Arun Subramanian called the episode a "wake-up call" and wrote, "While clients may demand the use of AI to save money, there is a risk of adverse consequences when things go wrong." The more proudly a firm shows off its AI, the more that AI's mistakes become the firm's.

"We need to earn that valuation everyday," Weinberg wrote in the December 2025 AMA.

**A status symbol only works while it's worth showing off. Harvey's next test is whether lawyers keep using what their firms bought.**

## What Transfers

Few companies sell to lawyers. Every B2B company sells to someone who has to defend the purchase.

**Win the customer everyone else copies, and help them announce it.** A&O's "first law firm" post did more for Harvey than anything Harvey said about itself. Pick a first customer whose name settles arguments, and make going first something they're proud to say.

**Demo on the buyer's own work.** A partner judging an argument against their own brief is engaged in a way no product tour can match. Personal demos take time, and AI makes them cheaper to build than ever.

**Give cautious buyers a precedent they can cite.** A respected firm that went first, a count of peers who followed, a benchmark against similar teams. Someone who has to defend a decision needs something to point to more than another feature.

**Sell through your customers to their customers.** Harvey's firms introduced it to clients, resold tools built on it and hosted clients inside it. In-house teams went from about 4% of revenue to 42% in 18 months.

**Expect every number you publish to be audited in public.** A running count builds momentum, and every figure in it will be questioned. Publish the ones you can defend line by line.

**When products converge, spend on the name.** If a rival can rebuild your app in two weeks, the brand is the part of the product your buyer has to defend in the meeting. Put money where they'll have to say it out loud.

Weinberg's whole strategy fits in one answer to Sequoia. Earn the trust of a few firms, and "the rest of them will trust you." Four years later, Harvey is worth $15.5 billion, and its most persuasive asset is still the list of firms that trusted it first.
