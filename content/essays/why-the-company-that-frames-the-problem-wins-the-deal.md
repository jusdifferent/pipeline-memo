A deal can start slipping away before the buyer compares a single product.

By the time vendors enter the conversation, most buyers have already settled on an explanation for what's wrong. A team frustrated with slow reporting decides it needs automation, without ever checking why the reports take so long to prepare.

That explanation shapes the competition. Once reporting speed is the priority, automation features and hours saved become the obvious scorecard. A vendor can spend the entire sales process proving it's the best answer to a diagnosis nobody tested.

This happens earlier than most teams assume. In 6sense's 2024 survey of more than 2,500 B2B buyers, buyers were nearly 70% of the way through their buying process before they talked to a seller. 85% had largely set their requirements, and 81% already had a preferred vendor when they first made contact (6sense, *2024 Buyer Experience Report*).

**How a buyer explains the problem decides what they shop for. Shape the explanation and you shape the deal.**

A company that helps buyers work out what's actually wrong gets to influence which capabilities matter, which alternatives belong in the evaluation, and what fixing the problem is worth. That advantage starts before any product comparison, and it lasts only as long as the explanation holds up.

## Most Vendors Compete Inside Someone Else's Framework

By the time a vendor enters an evaluation, the buyer has usually decided which capabilities matter and which compromises are acceptable.

Those priorities decide how much weight your strengths get. An advantage the buyer considers irrelevant drops out of the comparison entirely.

The framework might come from an incumbent, an executive mandate, a peer's recommendation, or another vendor's explanation. A competitor whose strengths fit those requirements can go straight to proving them. You carry an extra burden: explaining why something the buyer overlooked deserves attention at all.

And the process reinforces it. Demo questions, reference calls, and internal summaries all follow the same priorities. Each step produces more evidence about which vendor meets the requirements, and none of it tests whether the requirements address the real problem. By the time anyone challenges the diagnosis, revisiting it means reopening work the organization thought was finished.

**A comparison can be rigorous and still test the wrong thing.**

People don't judge options on their own. They judge them inside the frame the options arrive in. In a 1981 experiment, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described the same plan for responding to a disease outbreak two ways. Framed as 200 of 600 people being saved, it was chosen by 72% of participants. Framed as 400 of 600 people dying, the identical plan was chosen by 22% (Tversky & Kahneman, *Science*, 1981). The outcomes never changed. Only the description did.

A buyer's requirements document is a frame like that. Whoever wrote it has already decided what looks like a gain and what looks like a risk.

The useful question for sales and marketing is where your disadvantage comes from. If buyers understand the problem and your product falls short, changing the narrative won't fix a capability gap.

But if their criteria miss part of the problem, another feature comparison won't help. The next conversation has to examine the evidence behind those criteria, and whether meeting them would produce the outcome the buyer wants.

## A Better Diagnosis Changes the Competition

A better diagnosis can remove a competitor from the shortlist without disproving a single claim about its product.

The product does exactly what it promises. Those capabilities just matter less once the buyer understands what's actually blocking progress.

Take a software company evaluating sales-training providers because deals keep stalling. Conversations with lost prospects show that buyers understand the offer but fear a disruptive rollout. The real obstacle is implementation confidence. Guided onboarding, migration support, or a phased deployment deserves a look before another program for handling objections.

That changes the competitive set. Now leadership is comparing an outside service, an internal implementation project, and improvements to onboarding. Options that weren't in the original brief become credible because they address a cause the brief overlooked.

**A reframe matters commercially when it changes what counts as a solution.**

That's a bigger move than making your offer sound more compelling, because it changes the investment under consideration. One buyer who asked for a new tool discovers that an existing one is enough if the workflow changes. Another discovers that the process fix they expected can't get around a limit in their current technology.

The same thing happens at the scale of a whole market. In 2023, the data startup Clay put a reframe on its homepage: "Not another data provider. All of the data providers." Buyers had been comparing contact databases against each other. Clay changed the question to coverage across all of them. By 2025, Apollo and ZoomInfo had both shipped their own versions of Clay's multi-provider lookup. When competitors rebuild their product around your question, you've won the frame.

The direction has to follow the evidence. An honest diagnosis might show a problem your competitor solves better, or that no purchase is needed. Your advantage comes when you can show both why the original approach falls short and how yours addresses the actual constraint.

## A Problem Has to Earn Its Budget

A buyer can accept your diagnosis and still decline to fund the fix.

Your solution competes with everything else the buyer could spend the money on.

The business case gets stronger when you can trace specific symptoms to a specific cause, show what would resolve it, and estimate how much revenue it would realistically unlock.

Timing matters too. An upcoming expansion can make a workflow limit worth fixing now, because the same constraint will soon touch more customers. A planned system replacement can make an immediate purchase wasteful. The circumstances decide whether the problem deserves action, a limited test, or a deliberate delay. Urgency needs a reason the buyer can check.

This is where framing becomes useful to leadership. It connects a cause to a business consequence, shows what an intervention would change, and makes the tradeoff explicit. The buyer leaves with a defensible reason to move this project ahead of another one.

**A good frame wins the budget fight before the evaluation even starts.**

## Your Marketing Can Shape the Brief

Buyers spend surprisingly little of their buying time with vendors. Gartner's research puts it at 17%, split across every supplier they're considering. With three vendors in the running, that's roughly 5% to 6% each. Most of the rest goes to independent research and meetings with their own buying group (Gartner, *The B2B Buying Journey*).

So your marketing has to be useful while the buyer's judgments are forming. If you wait for discovery to introduce your diagnosis, it arrives after the buyer has already adopted someone else's.

Self-directed research still draws on sources. A comparison guide, a customer discussion, a research report, or a product walkthrough all suggest which capabilities matter and which tradeoffs deserve scrutiny.

When buyers find that reasoning useful, they carry its questions into internal discussions without ever talking to the company that published it. Your explanation matters when it helps them work out what a solution needs to do.

That gives paid social a specific job. An ad introduces a consequential question, and the content behind it helps buyers investigate the answer. An evaluation guide can explain when a capability matters, how to test whether it addresses the underlying problem, and when another approach would be better. Buyers should be able to make progress without a sales call standing between them and the substance.

**Your marketing can write the questions your product will eventually have to answer.**

The practical test is whether someone could use your content to build a better brief. Would they leave with a clearer diagnosis, a justified requirement, or a useful way to tell alternatives apart? That's a harder standard than making the product sound attractive.

Then use sales conversations to check whether it's working. Ask what shaped the buyer's requirements and why those criteria matter. Their answers show where your explanation helped, where another source was more persuasive, and where the buyer found something your marketing missed. That gives the next campaign something more useful than a new hook: a picture of how the evaluation is actually being built.

## Test the Diagnosis Before You Rewrite the Pitch

Before you change your messaging, examine the explanation buyers were using when they evaluated you.

Pull a small sample of recent wins, losses, and stalled deals. Read the initial requirements next to the discovery notes and final objections. What did the buyer believe was causing the problem? Which requirements followed from that belief? What evidence connected those requirements to the outcome they wanted?

The distinction to look for is an inconvenient scorecard versus a mistaken one. If buyers needed a capability you lacked, their criteria were probably right. If they understood your value but couldn't justify the cost of switching, the economics were against you. Neither is a reason to question their judgment just because you lost. Reframing is worth pursuing when the requirements leave an important part of the problem unsolved.

Pick one suspected mismatch and test it in buyer conversations before you build a campaign around it. Ask what led people to their current explanation, what they've already tried, and what evidence would make them reconsider the cause. If your alternative holds up, use it to improve an evaluation guide, a campaign, or a discovery conversation.

The signal you want is a buyer revising a requirement for a reason they can defend.

That's where I'd focus when strong demos keep failing to move an evaluation: find out whether the buyer is testing the right thing. If the diagnosis is sound, close the capability, commercial, or execution gap. If it's incomplete, help the buyer build better criteria before you present more evidence against the old ones.

**You earn influence over the buying criteria by improving the diagnosis behind them.**
